## How to use this doc

This is the working script for our final team presentation. Each slide section below contains four things: (1) what the slide should show, (2) who's speaking and the time target, (3) the verbatim script in italics — paste it directly into the speaker notes field of your assigned slide, and (4) which Part II rubric items it covers.

Scripts are written conversationally and timed at roughly 150 words per minute. Do one dry run before recording so the pacing feels right. If anything in here is wrong or you want to phrase your section differently, edit it directly — just keep the runtime under your target so we don't break the seven-minute cap (rubric \#1).

The narrative arc is intentional: the contextual inquiry from A3 carries the whole presentation. Slides 2 and 3 are about what we *learned* through field research, not just what we did. Every later design decision is anchored back to those observations.

## Slide 2 — The Activity We Observed

**Speaker: Peixin · \~60 sec** **Rubric: \#3 — and the foundational A3 contextual inquiry that everything else built on**

**On the slide:** Three or four real field-research photos — participant checking pantry, in-store at Trader Joe's or Kwik Trip, online Target browsing. A small caption strip beneath: "Kwik Trip · Trader Joe's · Capital Centre Market · Target app." Avoid text-heavy bullets; let the photos and Peixin's voice do the work.

**Speaker notes (paste into slide):**

*Our project started with contextual inquiry. The five of us shadowed UW–Madison students through their actual grocery routines — at Kwik Trip, Trader Joe's, Capital Centre Market, and online through the Target app. We sat with them as they checked their pantries, built lists on their phones, and made stop-by-stop decisions in the aisles. From these observations we learned something we didn't expect: grocery shopping for students isn't really one activity — it's at least four, layered together. They're simultaneously budgeting, planning transit, coordinating with roommates over text, and managing decision fatigue inside the store itself. One participant even routed herself between two stores on foot, deliberately, because the inconvenience kept her from impulse buying. That layering is what made the design challenge real — and it's where every later decision started.*

## Slide 3 — Insights from the Affinity Diagram

**Speaker: Angelique · \~60 sec** **Rubric: \#3 — also makes explicit which subset of the affinity themes we chose to address**

**On the slide:** Photo of the actual affinity diagram with the six theme clusters labeled. Visually highlight the three clusters we focused on (Behavioral Routines, Roommates / Coordination Overhead, Store Frustrations / Multi-stop Routing); leave the other three (Demographic Constraints, Planning, Quality/Brand) visible but de-emphasized. Caption underneath: "6 themes → 3 we built around."

**Speaker notes (paste into slide):**

*After our field work we built an affinity diagram together — clustering every observation into themes. Six themes surfaced: store frustrations, behavioral routines, demographic constraints like SNAP and transit, planning behaviors, the social context of roommates, and quality and brand preferences. Of those six, three patterns mattered most for the design we'd eventually build. The first surprised us: students don't actually deal-hunt — they build routines. They pick the same stores, brands, and days, because routine reduces cognitive load even more than chasing the cheapest price does. The second was that roommates create hidden coordination overhead — duplicate purchases and food waste happen when communication breaks down. The third was that price comparison and store-routing isn't optional — students are already doing this work in their heads, every week. From the affinity diagram we learned that our design's job wasn't to add features. It was to absorb the mental work students were already doing.*

## Slide 4 — The Problem We Distilled

**Speaker: Helena · \~50 sec** **Rubric: \#4**

**On the slide:** POV statement centered, with quotation marks framing it. Minimal text everywhere else. Let the slide breathe.

POV text:

*Budget-constrained students need simple ways to plan efficient grocery trips without increasing cognitive effort or time.*

**Speaker notes (paste into slide):**

*Those insights gave us a single point-of-view statement: budget-constrained students need simple ways to plan efficient grocery trips without increasing cognitive effort or time. The tension in that sentence is what made it useful. Students want thoughtful, cost-conscious decisions — but the process of getting there is exhausting. So our problem wasn't "help students save money." It was "help students save money without asking them to think harder." Every later design choice — from how we showed prices to how we labeled the bottom navigation — got tested against this lens: does it reduce mental effort, or does it just hide the effort somewhere new?*

## Slide 5 — Brainstorming and the Decision to Combine

**Speaker: Yanbin · \~70 sec** **Rubric: \#5 — and sets up \#6 by naming the pivot moment**

**On the slide:** Photo of the actual brainstorming board from A5 with the four themes labeled (Price · Collaboration · Meal Planning · Store Frustrations). To the right of the board, a small visual showing the three concept sketches converging into a single combined sketch with an arrow. Caption: "Three concepts → one integrated product."

**Speaker notes (paste into slide):**

*With our problem framed, we ran a brainstorming session around a How-Might-We prompt: how might we simplify grocery shopping so students can quickly find affordable food, plan meals, and stay within budget. We generated dozens of ideas and grouped them into four themes — Price, Collaboration, Meal Planning, and Store Frustrations — then dot-voted three concepts worth developing further: a price-comparison tool, collaborative roommate lists, and a meal-planning assistant. Here's where the project pivoted. When we each sketched one concept and ran a peer critique, we kept telling each other to combine features rather than pick one. The price tool needed roommate sharing; the meal planner needed pantry awareness; the collaboration tool needed budget tracking. So we made a deliberate choice: rather than picking one, we'd build the integrated version. From brainstorming and sketching we learned that the three concepts weren't competing — they were three angles on the same underlying problem.*

## Slide 6 — Design Evolution: Sketch → Storyboard → Prototype → Wireframe → Hi-Fi

**Speaker: Alejandro · \~65 sec** **Rubric: \#6 (prototype evolution); \#8 (course-concept justifications)**

**On the slide:** A horizontal four-stage progression strip with real artifact images:

1. Hand-drawn sketch (from A6)  
2. Storyboard frame \+ paper-prototype thumbnail (from A7)  
3. Wireframe screen (from A8)  
4. Hi-fi Figma screen

Caption each stage with one or two words: "Sketch · Storyboard \+ paper prototype · Wireframes · Hi-fi." No AI placeholder images — use the real artifacts.

**Speaker notes (paste into slide):**

*The integrated design went through four stages. We started with hand-drawn sketches that put meal planning, price comparison, and budget tracking onto a single screen — that consolidation directly attacked the cognitive-load problem we'd identified in observation. From there, we built storyboards and a low-fidelity paper prototype, which we tested with one student. The test showed that the meal-selection flow worked but the generate-list step caused hesitation — both findings carried forward. In the wireframes we locked in a four-item bottom navigation — Home, Lists, Dashboard, Groups — using the information-architecture principles from class: consistent labels, predictable hierarchy, and the same nav grid on every screen so users don't have to relearn the interface. In the hi-fi mockup we applied visual-design principles directly: contrast pulls the eye to the budget bar and the savings call-out, list patterns stay consistent across screens, and a single accent color signals positive financial outcomes — savings and in-budget status. Every visual decision traces back to either an observation insight or an information-architecture principle from the course.*

## Slide 7 — Final Mockup, How It Solves the Problem, Next Steps

**Speakers: Helena (mockup walkthrough) → Angelique (next steps) · \~85 sec total** **Rubric: \#7 (final mockups); \#9 (how the design solves the problem); \#11 (mockup link \+ task description)**

**On the slide:** Three or four hi-fi mockup screens shown together — Home / List Builder, Optimized Grocery List, Map, Dashboard, plus a small Groups thumbnail. Below the screens: the Figma prototype URL and the demo task in plain text.

**Demo task text on the slide (must be visible — rubric \#11):**

*Import a recipe and complete an optimized two-store trip while staying inside your monthly budget.*

### Helena's speaker notes (\~60 sec):

*Here's where we landed. A student opens GroceryPal, builds a list — by typing, importing a recipe, or pulling from history — and the app does what students were already doing in their heads: it splits items into the cheapest combination of stores, shows the savings, and routes the trip on a map. The dashboard keeps the full grocery total visible against a monthly budget, so cost stays salient at every step. And the Groups tab lets roommates share a pantry, claim items, and chat — so duplicate purchases stop at the source. This solves the problem we started with directly: GroceryPal absorbs the comparison, routing, coordination, and budgeting work students were already doing in their heads — they get the financial benefit without the cognitive cost. The interactive Figma prototype is linked on the slide, and the demo task is: import a recipe and complete an optimized two-store trip while staying inside your monthly budget.*

### Angelique's speaker notes (\~25 sec):

*With more time, three next steps. First, usability testing with SNAP-eligible students and students who rely on transit, so the routing feature reflects real constraints — not just optimal pricing. Second, pantry-aware recommendations so the app remembers what you already have at home. And third, integrating a real-time price API so the comparisons stay accurate. Thanks for listening.*
